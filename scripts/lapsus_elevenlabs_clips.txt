=== CLIP 1 ===
September, twenty twenty two. A budget hotel room in England. A seventeen year old boy is staying here under police protection.

He is already on bail for hacking some of the biggest companies on Earth. His laptop has been taken away. He is banned from owning a smartphone.

So he uses what he can get. An Amazon Fire Stick. The hotel television. And a phone he is not supposed to have.

With that, he breaks into the studio making the most anticipated video game in history. And he leaves a message for every employee to see.

I am not a Rockstar employee. I am an attacker.

And this wasn't even his biggest hack.

=== CLIP 2 ===
That weekend, ninety video clips appeared on a gaming forum. Almost an hour of early, unfinished footage from Grand Theft Auto Six.

Within hours it was everywhere. One of the biggest leaks in the history of video games.

But Rockstar was only the latest name on a very long list. In about a year, the same group had broken into Nvidia. Samsung. Microsoft. Uber.

Investigators assumed they were hunting a foreign government. A professional army of hackers.

They were wrong. The trail led to a teenager's bedroom in England. And a group that called itself Lapsus.

And this month, more than four years later, that teenager finally stood up in court... and said one word. Guilty.

=== CLIP 3 ===
To understand how we got here, you have to start near Oxford. With a boy named Arion Kurtaj.

Arion is autistic, and he went to a special school. In the real world, things were hard. Online, he was somebody else.

Online, he was known as White. Or Breachbase. And he was very, very good with computers.

He found his way into a corner of the internet where teenagers trade tricks for breaking into accounts. A world where your reputation depends on how big your last hack was.

And in that world, White was climbing fast.

By twenty twenty one, while most kids his age were sitting exams, he already had his first convictions.

=== CLIP 4 ===
The trick that made his name doesn't need any brilliant code. It's called SIM swapping.

Think about how many of your accounts send a code to your phone. Your bank. Your email. Your crypto. Your phone number is the master key to your whole life.

So the attacker goes after the phone company. They trick an employee. Or bribe one. Or break into the company's own tools.

And they move your number onto a SIM card they control. Now every code goes to them. They reset your passwords, and they walk straight in.

No secret software. Often, just a convincing voice on the phone.

=== CLIP 5 ===
In the summer of twenty twenty one, White and his associates aimed that trick at something much bigger. BT, the British telecoms giant, and its mobile network, EE.

They got inside the company's systems. And once they were in, more than nine hundred phone numbers were quietly moved onto SIM cards the hackers controlled.

Then the money started to disappear. Cash and cryptocurrency, taken straight out of customers' accounts.

And on August first, they sent the company a demand. Four million dollars... or the stolen data gets released.

BT didn't pay.

It didn't matter. White had learned something more valuable than four million dollars. He had learned that it worked.

=== CLIP 6 ===
On January twenty fourth, twenty twenty two, the police came for him.

They arrested Arion and searched the house. They took phones. Laptops. USB sticks. A pile of SIM cards.

He was questioned. And then he was released while detectives worked through the evidence.

He was sixteen years old. His devices were gone. The police knew his name.

Most people would have stopped right there.

Within weeks, he was part of something far bigger. A hack on one of the most valuable companies in the world.

He was just getting started.

=== CLIP 7 ===
Because by now, White had new friends. A loose crew of young hackers who had found each other online. They gave themselves a name. Lapsus.

And they did something almost no criminal group does. They went public. They opened a channel on a messaging app, and invited the whole world to watch.

They posted screenshots from inside their victims' systems. They taunted companies by name. They joked with their fans.

Most hackers work in silence, and hope nobody ever learns their name. Lapsus wanted an audience.

And they got one. Reporters joined the channel. So did security researchers. So, quietly, did the police.

=== CLIP 8 ===
So how does a group of teenagers break into some of the best defended companies on the planet?

Step one. Buy a password. Stolen employee logins are sold online, sometimes for just a few dollars.

Or skip that, and buy the employee. Lapsus openly advertised for insiders. Staff at phone companies and tech firms, willing to sell their access.

But a password isn't enough. Most companies also make you approve the login on your phone. So Lapsus sent the request again. And again. And again. In the middle of the night.

Until somebody, half asleep, just wanted it to stop... and tapped yes.

That's it. That was the whole trick.

=== CLIP 9 ===
In February, twenty twenty two, Lapsus went after Nvidia. The company whose chips power video games, and now artificial intelligence.

They took nearly a terabyte of data. Chip designs. Internal software. Employee logins.

Then they announced it to their followers, and waited for the company to respond.

When the ransom demand came, it was not what anyone expected.

They didn't ask for money. They asked Nvidia to change its graphics cards... so they'd be better at mining cryptocurrency.

And then they demanded the company publish the software behind its chips. For free. For everyone. Forever.

It was a ransom note that read like a teenager's wish list.

=== CLIP 10 ===
Nvidia refused. So Lapsus started leaking. And March, twenty twenty two, turned into the wildest month the security world had seen in years.

March fourth. Samsung. A hundred and ninety gigabytes of data, including source code for its Galaxy phones. Dumped online for anyone to download.

March twenty first. Microsoft. Lapsus posted source code from Bing, from Bing Maps, and from its voice assistant, Cortana.

Three of the biggest technology companies on Earth, in three weeks. By a group that announced every move in a public chat.

Inside every large company, security teams were now watching that channel, and asking the same question. Are we next?

And then came the one that scared everybody.

=== CLIP 11 ===
There's a company most people have never heard of, called Okta. It doesn't make phones or games. It runs the login page for thousands of other companies.

Banks. Hospitals. Governments. Break into the company that guards the doors... and you might get every door at once.

On March twenty second, Lapsus posted screenshots from inside Okta's systems.

Security teams everywhere dropped what they were doing. Nobody knew how far it went.

The truth was smaller, and stranger. Lapsus had got in through a single laptop. It belonged to a support worker at an outside contractor. They'd had access for five days, back in January.

In the end, only two customers were affected. But the lesson landed. If teenagers could get that close to the master key... nothing was safe.

=== CLIP 12 ===
So what was the point of all this?

Sometimes they asked for money. But investigators kept noticing the same thing. This group wasn't behaving like a business. It was behaving like it wanted to be famous.

In some attacks, the hackers didn't just steal the data and leave. They stayed. They slipped into the company's own emergency calls, and listened to the staff trying to throw them out.

They read the internal chats about themselves. As it was happening.

Then they went back to their channel, and told their followers all about it.

They weren't hiding. They were performing. And everyone was watching. Including some people White had made very angry.

=== CLIP 13 ===
Because the year before, White had bought a website. A notorious one, called Doxbin. A place for posting people's private information. Their names. Their addresses.

By most accounts, he ran it badly. The site's users turned on him. In January, he gave up control of it.

But on his way out, he leaked the site's own data.

That was a mistake. He had just picked a fight with a community whose entire hobby... was exposing people.

So they exposed him. His real name. His home address. Details about his family.

They even claimed he was sitting on a fortune in Bitcoin. That number has never been confirmed.

The boy who exposed companies... had been exposed himself.

=== CLIP 14 ===
Security researchers had been quietly tracking White for months. They'd connected his usernames. His habits. His mistakes.

And on March twenty third, twenty twenty two, the story went public. The suspected leader of Lapsus was a sixteen year old. Living with his mother. Near Oxford.

The world's most talked about hacking group, traced to a teenager's bedroom.

One day later, British police announced seven arrests. All of them between sixteen and twenty one years old.

And on March thirtieth, Arion was arrested again. For the second time in ten weeks.

This time he was charged. And this time, the court set rules.

=== CLIP 15 ===
He was released on bail, with strict conditions. He was not allowed to own a smartphone.

But there was a second problem. His home address had been posted online by his rivals. And those rivals were not finished with him.

So police moved him. Out of his home, and into a budget hotel. For his own protection.

No laptop. No computer. Just a bed, a kettle... and a television on the wall.

For months, it seemed to work. Lapsus went quiet. The channel stopped posting. The headlines moved on.

But inside that hotel room, a boy with nothing to do, and one very particular talent... was getting bored.

=== CLIP 16 ===
September, twenty twenty two. Uber. One of the biggest ride hailing companies in the world.

The attacker had a contractor's password, bought online. Then came the flood of login requests to the contractor's phone.

When that didn't work, the attacker sent a message pretending to be Uber's own tech support. Just approve it, and the alerts will stop.

The contractor approved it. And the attacker was inside.

Then came the announcement. A message posted in Uber's own company chat, telling the staff they had been hacked.

The banking app Revolut was hit in the same spree.

And a few days later... the boy in the hotel room picked a new target.

=== CLIP 17 ===
From that hotel room, using a Fire Stick, a television, and a phone, Arion got into the internal chat of Rockstar Games. The studio behind Grand Theft Auto.

He sent a message to every employee. I am not a Rockstar employee. I am an attacker.

And he threatened to release the game's source code, unless the company contacted him.

Rockstar didn't make a deal.

So, under the name Teapot Uber Hacker, he posted ninety clips of the unfinished game on a fan forum.

Rockstar confirmed it. Years of secret work, seen by the entire world in its roughest, most unfinished form.

=== CLIP 18 ===
On September twenty second, twenty twenty two, police walked into that hotel room.

They found the Fire Stick. And they found an iPhone. The one thing his bail conditions said he could not have.

Look at that timeline. Arrested in January. Released. Arrested in March. Bailed. Arrested in September.

And between every single arrest... another hack.

This time, there was no bail. He was held in custody to wait for trial.

The channel fell silent. The leaks stopped. Lapsus, as the world knew it, was finished.

He was seventeen years old.

=== CLIP 19 ===
The trial began in London, in the summer of twenty twenty three. And it started with an unusual ruling.

Doctors had assessed Arion. Because of his autism, they found he was not fit to stand trial.

So the jury was given a different job. Not to decide whether he was guilty. Only to decide whether he had done the things he was accused of.

For weeks, they heard the evidence. The ransom demand. The nine hundred phone numbers. The hotel room.

In August, the jury gave its answer. He had carried out twelve offences. Blackmail. Fraud. Computer misuse.

A second teenager, seventeen years old, was found guilty of his part in the attacks.

=== CLIP 20 ===
In December, twenty twenty three, the judge passed sentence. And it wasn't prison.

Arion was given an indefinite hospital order. He would be held in a secure hospital. Not for a fixed number of years. For as long as he was judged to be a danger.

The judge said his skills, and his desire to keep committing cybercrime, meant he remained a high risk to the public.

Doctors had reported that he was still saying it. He wanted to go back to hacking as soon as he could.

The younger defendant avoided custody. He was given a youth rehabilitation order, with eighteen months of supervision.

Arion was eighteen years old. And nobody could say when he would be released.

=== CLIP 21 ===
For Rockstar, the leak was a nightmare. The company told the court it cost five million dollars to recover, plus thousands of hours of its staff's time.

And it changed how the studio worked. Security was tightened across the company.

But here's the strange part. A little over a year after the leak, Rockstar released the first official trailer.

It was watched more than ninety million times in a single day.

The leak didn't kill the hype. His own lawyers even pointed to the trailer's success in court. The judge was not persuaded. Real companies, and real people, had been hurt.

=== CLIP 22 ===
In the United States, the government ordered an official review. How had a group of teenagers done all this?

The answer was uncomfortable. Lapsus hadn't used secret weapons. They used cheap, simple techniques. Tricks that had been known about for years.

A phone call. A bribe. A login request sent a hundred times.

The review singled out one weakness above all. Login codes sent by text message. It told companies to stop relying on them.

Companies had spent billions on firewalls and encryption. And the hackers walked around all of it... by asking a tired human to say yes.

The weakest part of any system isn't the code. It's a person.

=== CLIP 23 ===
If you've watched this channel before, that should sound familiar.

Silk Road fell because of an old email address. AlphaBay fell because of an old email address. And Lapsus fell because its leader couldn't resist picking a fight.

Think about it. They broke into Nvidia, Samsung and Microsoft. And they were undone by bragging in a public chat room, and by making enemies on a forum.

And the playbook didn't die with them. Other young hacking crews have copied the same tricks since. The phone calls. The fake tech support. The flood of login prompts.

Even the second teenager from the trial went back to it. He was later sentenced to five and a half years for new cybercrimes.

=== CLIP 24 ===
But Arion's story wasn't over. From the secure hospital, his lawyers went to the Court of Appeal.

They argued the jury should never have been told certain things. About his earlier convictions. About his bail conditions.

In September, twenty twenty five, the judges gave their decision. The jury's findings would stand.

Then came a bigger change. In January, twenty twenty six, doctors assessed him again. And this time, they found he was fit to face trial after all.

He was moved out of the hospital. And into a regular prison.

Three years after the first trial... the case was back on.

=== CLIP 25 ===
On November sixteenth, twenty twenty six, Arion Kurtaj stood in a London courtroom. He was twenty one years old.

And this time, there was no jury to decide for him. He pleaded guilty. To the Rockstar hack. To the attack on Uber. To the crimes he committed as a teenager with Lapsus.

And the timing is almost unbelievable. The very same week... Grand Theft Auto Six finally went on sale.

The game he leaked from a hotel television, four years earlier, arrived in shops. While he waited in a cell to hear his sentence.

=== CLIP 26 ===
So what do we make of Arion Kurtaj?

To some people, he's a dangerous criminal. He blackmailed companies. He helped drain strangers' accounts. And he didn't stop when he was caught. Not the first time. Not the second.

To others, he was a vulnerable teenager. Autistic. Isolated. Pulled into an online world that rewarded him for every bigger, riskier hack... until there was no way back.

Maybe both are true.

So what's the right answer for a brilliant kid who couldn't stop? Prison? Hospital? A job in cybersecurity?
